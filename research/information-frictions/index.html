When job information travels through different networks, do workers see different wages?

This research design studies whether Japanese/formal and Chinese/co-ethnic job-search channels expose Chinese international students in Japan to different sets of part-time opportunities—and whether those differences appear in hourly wages.

Kaibo Tang · School of Economics, The University of Osaka

01 · Research question

Does the way a job is found shape the wage eventually received?

Job-search channels do more than transmit vacancies. They can change search costs, trust, language requirements, and the range of employers a student encounters. The design asks whether these information environments are associated with different part-time wage offers.

02

Contrasting information environments are defined before analysis.

01

Search friction

Native-language contacts may make trustworthy job information faster and cheaper to obtain.

02

Opportunity set

Different channels may reveal different employers, occupations, locations, and wage offers.

03

Labor-market sorting

Language ability, geography, and job type may jointly shape both channel choice and wages.

02 · Information environments

Two channels, one deliberately balanced comparison.

The contrast is not between a “good” and a “bad” network. Co-ethnic networks can provide trust, speed, and protection; formal channels can broaden the searchable market. The empirical question is whether their opportunity sets differ.
01

Broader formal search

Japanese / formal

Japanese job platforms, university boards, store notices, and direct applications.
02

Lower-friction search

Chinese / co-ethnic

WeChat, Xiaohongshu, Chinese-speaking acquaintances, and ethnic business networks.
03

Mechanism—not a finding

Information tax

A hypothesized hidden wage cost if lower search friction also narrows the set of visible jobs and wage offers.
i

“Information tax” is a hypothesis to be tested, not a reported result. The study is designed to avoid stigmatizing Chinese-language or co-ethnic networks.

03 · Survey design

The unit of analysis is a student–job match.

Eligible respondents are Chinese international students enrolled in Japanese universities, graduate schools, language schools, or vocational schools who have part-time work experience in Japan. The preferred reference is the respondent’s current main part-time job.
Target population

Chinese international students with part-time work experience in Japan

Baseline coding

Mixed and other search channels are excluded from the main contrast and restored in alternative-coding checks.

01 · Outcome

Log hourly wage

Hourly earnings in yen, transformed to the natural logarithm.

02 · Main exposure

JapaneseChannel

1 for Japanese/formal channels; 0 for Chinese/co-ethnic channels.

03 · Candidate instrument

ChineseAreaTime

Travel time from residence to the nearest Chinese-community or Chinese-supermarket area.

04 · Pre-specified controls

Language, gender & place

Japanese proficiency, gender, prefecture/city, and distance to the city center.

Survey modules cover employment, search channels, Japanese ability, location, background, and awareness of job information.

04 · Pre-analysis ladder

Five models separate description, adjustment, identification, and mechanism.

The sequence is fixed in advance so that increasingly demanding claims are not collapsed into a single preferred specification.
01

Model 1

Raw association

Bivariate channel–wage relationship with no controls.
02

Model 2

Core adjustment

Adds Japanese proficiency and gender.
03

Model 3

Main OLS

Adds geography and urban centrality; interpreted as associational.
04

Model 4

Candidate IV / 2SLS

Uses ChineseAreaTime to predict Japanese-channel use, subject to first-stage and exclusion diagnostics.
05

Model 5

Robustness & mechanism

Adds commute time, school type, and job type; attenuation after job-type controls is treated as evidence consistent with occupational sorting.
Design status

This version reports the design only. It contains no sample estimates, coefficient signs, significance tests, or causal results.

05 · Identification strategy

Spatial exposure is a candidate instrument—not a shortcut to causality.

The proposed instrument uses travel time to a Chinese-community or Chinese-supermarket area as a source of variation in access to co-ethnic job information. Every link in that logic is treated as an empirical or substantive assumption.
01Residential proximity→
02Network exposure→
03Search-channel choice→
04Hourly wage
01

Relevance

Longer travel time should predict greater use of Japanese/formal channels. The first-stage coefficient and F statistic must be reported.

02

Exclusion restriction

Travel time may also proxy urban centrality, transit access, rent, and local job density. These alternative pathways make the exclusion restriction contestable.

03

Interpretation rule

A weak first stage means the IV estimate will not be presented as a headline result; OLS remains explicitly associational.

Pre-specified hypothesis

The design predicts a Japanese-channel wage premium, but the magnitude, persistence after controls, and any IV estimate remain open empirical questions.

06 · Limits & research ethics

The strongest version of the study is transparent about what it cannot identify.

Cross-sectional data, self-selection into search channels, mixed-channel classification, spatial confounding, and unobserved heterogeneity constrain interpretation. External validity is limited to Chinese international students in Japan.
01

Cross-sectional design

The design cannot establish temporal ordering as cleanly as a randomized or longitudinal study.

02

Channel measurement

Respondents may use multiple channels, so baseline and alternative coding rules must be reported together.

03

Spatial instrument

Geographic proximity can affect wages through pathways other than information networks.

04

External validity

Results should not be generalized beyond the defined student population without further evidence.

Privacy & non-stigmatization

The survey avoids precise home addresses and unnecessary employer names, separates geography from identifiers, permits skipped questions, aggregates reporting, and states that responses will not be shared with universities, employers, or public authorities.
